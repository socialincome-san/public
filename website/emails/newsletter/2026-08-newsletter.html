Beyond Growth

Hundreds of economists — Nobel Prize winners included — published a letter arguing that growth as a strategy is finished, and that poverty and inequality are policy choices, not accidents of fate. I know, policy debates aren't exactly gripping reading.

But when the people who built the models start tearing them down, that's worth a moment of your attention.

Vote Money

America's richest 0.1% now hold more wealth than China's entire GDP. According to economist Robert Reich, the real danger isn't just the widening wealth gap — it's that extreme wealth buys political power, creating a cycle where money makes more money.

A healthy reminder that cash transfers don't just reduce poverty; they help rebalance opportunity before inequality starts rewriting the rules

Goat economics

Another day, another study proving that cash has a habit of multiplying: New research on INclusion Worldwide's programme in Northern Uganda found that recipients built significantly more wealth, accumulating land, livestock, and other productive assets long after the transfers arrived.

The money moves on, but it leaves something behind.

My World in Data

3.4 billion

If your household spent more on debt interest than on health or education, you'd probably call it unsustainable. Yet 3.4 billion people live in countries where that's exactly how public finances work, with governments spending nearly US$1.4 trillion a year on debt repayments.

Cash transfers fight poverty. Debt relief would give governments the cash to do the same.

3.7 billion

I've never quite warmed to the idea that poverty can be captured by a single dollar figure. Yet even using the World Bank's highest international poverty line - $8.30 a day - 3.7 billion people would still be considered poor.

Proof that sometimes a statistic raises more questions than it answers.
