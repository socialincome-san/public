Stable what?

No, not horses, although Anna, one of our newest team members, has one. I mean stablecoins: digital currencies designed to keep a stable value. We tested USDC transfers to Ghana ourselves and cut our transfer costs in half. Wow! And the WTO, where I work by day, is also taking notice: my colleagues just published a report on stablecoins and world trade.

If you hadn’t heard of stablecoins before, you have now. And you’ll probably hear a lot more about them, including from us.

Forced return

I’ve written before about US deals with West African countries to receive deportees, including Liberia and Sierra Leone. A new Guardian investigation tells the stories of deportees, among them Rabbiatu Kuyateh, a Sierra Leonean who had lived in the US for decades before being deported back to the country she fled as a child. She was dragged, shoeless and crying, into the waiting van, protesting: “I’m not going.”

Cash, please

Ask people in a crisis how they want to be helped and they'll tell you: cash. That's 79% in Ukraine, 89% in Afghanistan and 98% in Myanmar, according to a new analysis by the CALP Network. Still, cash has been stuck at 22% of humanitarian aid for years.

The people know. The system doesn't.

My World in Data

10.2 trillion

That's how many dollars in stablecoin transactions moved over the past year. Mobile money? $2 trillion. That's a lot of money moving without much regard for banks, borders and currencies. For emerging markets, that could make remittances and cash transfers cheaper and faster.

Banks, meanwhile, still close at five.

37.5%

What if the same humanitarian funding could reach millions more people? A global study estimates that $1.1–3.3 billion could be used more efficiently each year. This is enough to reach up to 60 million more people in crisis. One finding stands out: cash assistance was 37.5% more efficient than food aid.

Same money, more people.

1 in 3

That's how much of Europe's cocaine may now pass through West Africa, with Sierra Leone as a major transit point, according to a new Economist investigation. The allegations of corruption reach into politics, security and business.

Poverty makes for cheap recruits.

$700

That's what some of Malawi's poorest people are each receiving from Canva's co-founders, as part of a $150 million pledge in no-strings-attached cash. Their philosophy? " There is enough money, goodwill, and good intentions in the world to solve most of the world's problems."

Optimism with a budget. Partly funded by my Canva subscription.
